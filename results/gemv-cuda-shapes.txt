CUDA GEMV across shapes: the L2 crossover
=========================================

Every earlier kernel conclusion was drawn at ONE shape (N=131072 K=8192,
256 MiB of weights). Re-running across shapes shows the best kernel
DEPENDS on whether the weight matrix fits Thor's 32 MB L2, and the two
regimes prefer opposite designs.

CROSSOVER SWEEP (K=5120, growing N, Q2_0, GB/s of weight bytes)
---------------------------------------------------------------
  weights MiB      N       v5 (1 row)   v7 (2 rows)   winner
        5        4096         212.4        254.8        v7
       20       16384         237.9        308.6        v7
       40       32768         212.6        204.5        v5
       80       65536         217.1        207.9        v5
      160      131072         220.5        211.3        v5
      320      262144         177.6        169.9        v5

The flip happens between 20 and 40 MiB, i.e. exactly at the 32 MB L2.

REAL BONSAI MATRIX SHAPES (hidden 5120, intermediate 17408)
-----------------------------------------------------------
  shape              MiB    v3      v5      v7x2    v7x4    v7x8
  K=5120  N=5120       6   159.9   214.5   264.5
  K=5120  N=17408     21   172.5   241.0   310.8
  K=17408 N=5120      21   177.3   310.9   387.8   349.9   305.7

Note 387.8 GB/s EXCEEDS the 273 GB/s DRAM spec. That is not an error and
not a win -- it is the tell that these matrices are being served from L2.

WHICH REGIME IS REAL? THE STREAMING ONE.
----------------------------------------
Every individual Bonsai matrix (6-21 MiB) fits in L2, so it is tempting
to read the 264-388 GB/s numbers as the real decode rate. They are not.

A decode step traverses all 64 layers, ~3.8 GB of weights for the 1-bit
build, and reads each matrix EXACTLY ONCE. There is no reuse to exploit:
by the time the next token needs layer 0 again, the intervening 3.8 GB
has evicted it many times over. The benchmark's 30 iterations over one
resident matrix manufacture a locality that decode does not have.

So the honest decode proxy is the >32 MB column, where v5 wins and lands
at 178-220 GB/s. The L2-resident figures describe a regime that only
occurs in a microbenchmark.

CORRECTION TO OUR OWN DESIGN RULE
---------------------------------
We previously concluded (WIKI K2b) that "row-blocking is wrong for this
kernel" and that the kernel is "issue-bound and occupancy-sensitive, not
latency-bound." That is right in the STREAMING regime and wrong in the
L2-resident one, where v7 beats v5 by 23-30%.

The physical reading: when weights come from DRAM, the kernel is limited
by bandwidth and by occupancy, and extra rows per warp cost registers
without buying bandwidth. When weights come from L2 -- which is ~4x
faster than DRAM here -- bandwidth is no longer the limit, and the
activation reuse that rows-per-warp provides starts to pay.

Stated as a rule: rows-per-warp buys ACTIVATION REUSE, which is only
worth having once WEIGHT bandwidth has stopped being the bottleneck.

POSSIBLE RESOLUTION OF OPEN QUESTION Q2
---------------------------------------
Q2 asks why we measure 178 GB/s against a reported 229 GB/s (84% of
spec). A shape with any L2 residency reaches 229 easily here: v5 alone
hits 237.9 GB/s at 20 MiB, and v7 hits 308.6. So a 229 GB/s figure is
fully consistent with a measurement that had L2 reuse, and inconsistent
with our streaming measurements.

This is a HYPOTHESIS, not a confirmation -- the reported figure was
attributed to a 256 MiB shape, where we measure 177.6. But it is the
first explanation that fits the numbers, and it predicts something
checkable: any re-measurement must state the weight footprint relative
to L2, or the number is not interpretable.

HOW CLOSE IS v5 TO THE MACHINE? 90%.
------------------------------------
Measured achievable streaming read on this device (bench/bandwidth):

  128-bit (float4) loads : 244.7 GB/s
  32-bit  (uint32) loads : 161.4 GB/s

v5 in the streaming regime reaches 212-220 GB/s (40-160 MiB shapes), so
it is at ~90% of achievable read bandwidth. There is very little headroom
left in this kernel.

Two things worth noting:

1. v5 uses 32-bit weight loads yet beats the 161.4 GB/s scalar-read
   figure, reaching 220. That is not a contradiction -- v5's loads are
   coalesced across the warp (lane i reads word i, so a warp covers 128
   contiguous bytes), which the memory system merges into wide
   transactions. The 161.4 number characterizes a non-coalescing scalar
   pattern and is not a ceiling for well-formed 32-bit access. It also
   explains why v6's explicit uint4 loads bought nothing: the hardware
   was already coalescing.

2. Throughput falls to 177.6 GB/s at 320 MiB, below the 212-220 seen at
   40-160 MiB. Unexplained; TLB or page-level effects are the obvious
   suspects. Real Bonsai matrices are 6-21 MiB so this regime is not on
   the critical path, but it is why the single-shape 256 MiB benchmark
   understated the kernel.

CORRECTED ROOFLINE
------------------
Using ~215 GB/s as the fair streaming rate for this kernel rather than
the 177.6 measured at one oversized shape:

  ternary Q2_0 : 7.165 GB/token -> ~30 tok/s ceiling
  reference fork measures 16.77 -> we are at ~56% of it

So the honest headroom from kernel work is about 1.8x, up from the 1.55x
implied by the 256 MiB figure, and against the 244.7 GB/s machine limit
the absolute ceiling is ~34 tok/s.

WAVE QUANTIZATION AT SMALL N -- split-K is justified
----------------------------------------------------
v5 assigns one row per warp, 8 warps per block, so the grid is N/8
blocks. Thor has 20 SMs and holds ~6 blocks of 256 threads each, i.e.
~120 concurrent blocks. Small-N matrices therefore cannot fill the
machine:

  N       blocks   waves    v5 GB/s   % of large-N
  512        64     0.53      96.7        46%
  1024      128     1.07     127.9        60%
  2048      256     2.13     176.4        83%
  5120      640     5.33     212.2       100%

At N=512 barely half the machine is occupied and throughput is 46% of
what the same kernel achieves at N=5120.

This matters for real layers, not just synthetic ones. Bonsai's
full-attention layers have 4 KV heads x 256 head_dim = 1024-row K and V
projections, which land squarely in the degraded region at ~60%.

So splitting K across CTAs -- the one untried CUDA lever we had not
justified -- is now justified, and specifically for the small-N
projections rather than as a general optimization. The large matmuls
(N >= 5120) are already at ~5 waves and need nothing.

SPLIT-K: IMPLEMENTED AND REJECTED
---------------------------------
The wave-quantization table above motivated split-K, so it was built
(v8: each block owns (row, k-chunk), atomicAdd into a zeroed output,
staging only its own slice of activations). Measured at N=1024, the real
KV-projection shape:

  v5 dp4a + smem acts     128.1 GB/s
  v7 smem acts + 2 rows   155.3 GB/s
  v8 split-K x4            77.7 GB/s
  v8 split-K x8            52.1 GB/s

Split-K is roughly half the speed of doing nothing, and it gets worse
with more splits.

Two honest caveats on that measurement. First, the required
`cudaMemsetAsync` sits inside the timed region; at ~3 us of launch
overhead against a 17 us kernel that is worth perhaps 20%, which does not
come close to closing a 128 vs 78 gap. Second, N=1024 x K=5120 is 2.5
MiB and therefore L2-resident, so this is the regime where v7 wins
anyway.

The more interesting result is that v7 -- which has FEWER blocks than v5
(N/16 vs N/8) -- is the fastest thing at N=1024. That is the opposite of
what the occupancy argument predicts, and it means the small-N slowdown
is not purely wave quantization. At these sizes the matrix is L2-resident
and activation reuse dominates, exactly as the crossover sweep showed.

PRACTICAL CONCLUSION: SHAPE-ADAPTIVE SELECTION
----------------------------------------------
Rather than one kernel plus split-K, the engine should pick by footprint:

  weights < ~32 MB (L2-resident)   -> v7, rows per warp
  weights > ~32 MB (streaming)     -> v5, one row per warp

For a real decode step every matrix is read once across ~3.8 GB, so the
streaming rule governs and v5 is the default. The v7 path is worth
keeping for any case where a matrix genuinely stays resident -- small
models, or a deliberately pinned tensor.
